# A streaming sieve for divisor pairs in a clipped interval

For positive integers $1\le X\le Z$ and $1\le A\le B$, the program reports every $(n,x,y)$ with $X\le n=xy\le Z$ and $A\le x\le B$, in increasing order of $(n,x)$. With the default column interval $[1,Z]$, it lists all divisors of every integer in the product interval. To obtain prime multiplicities, strip each arriving divisor greater than one from the residual until it ceases to divide. A composite divisor has already had each of its prime divisors stripped. Initialization needs only $X$ and the column bounds.

Let $K$ be the number of vertices on the initial clipped lower hull, $M$ the number of output triples, and $b=\lceil\log_2(2+\max(Z,B))\rceil$. Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, the implementation takes
$$
O((K+1)b^4+(M+1)b^3)
$$
bit operations. After initialization it jumps directly from one product to the next represented product. Its working storage is $O((K_{\max}+1)b+b^2)$ bits in a word-addressed representation with $O(b)$-bit pointers, where $K_{\max}$ is the largest live hull. Output can be consumed as a stream. [Implementation](hyperbola_hull_sieve.py)

### Initialization

Take the integer hull of $A\le x\le B,\ y>0,\ xy\ge X$. Its two ends have upward vertical rays. Start at $(A,\lceil X/A\rceil)$. From a vertex $P$ with $P_x<B$, find the minimum slope to a feasible point strictly to its right and take the farthest lattice point at that slope. This generates the clipped hull, including both endpoints.

The code implements the integer-lattice specialization of the Stern–Brocot search in Algorithm 2 of Alcántara, Blanco, Criado and Santos. The extra wall $x\le B$ is included in every ray intersection. The correctness argument uses convexity as in their Remark 3.22. In particular, for a unimodular search basis $u,v$ with $P+u$ feasible, a feasible vector $i u+j v$ for $i,j\ge1$ implies that the ray $P+v+k u$ contains a feasible integer point. At $v$-coordinate 1 the triangle with vertices $P,P+u,P+i u+j v$ contains $k=\lceil i/j\rceil$. This argument also holds at the wall. The search starts with $u=(1,0),v=(0,-1)$, so $P+v$ is outside at the bottom point of each vertex column. If a lower point in that column existed it would lie below the supporting line.

Along each candidate ray the product is a concave quadratic or a linear function. Restrict first to the integer parameter interval allowed by $x\le B,\ y\ge1$, find its maximum at an endpoint or an integer nearest the stationary point, and bisect both monotone sides for the inclusive feasible interval. This handles exact roots and empty intervals. A search iteration takes $O(b)$ operations on $O(b)$-bit integers. After the initial step, each nonterminal iteration adds a positive multiple of each basis vector to the other in turn. Their coordinate lengths, measured as $d_x-d_y$, grow at least as Fibonacci numbers. They stay $O(B+X)$ because each updated inside point is feasible, and the updated outside point immediately precedes an entry on a rightward, downward ray. There are $O(b)$ iterations per vertex. The one-column case directly streams its multiples. [Hyperbola hull paper, Algorithm 2 and Remark 3.22](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19193)

### Advancing the product

Store the vertices in boundary order in a doubly linked list and also in a minimum heap keyed by $(xy,x)$. Among remaining lattice points, every point of least product $m$ is an exposed vertex. Indeed, at a point $(d,m/d)$, the linear functional $(m/d)x+dy$ is at least $2m$ throughout $xy\ge m$, with equality only at that point. Thus extracting the minimum heap entry reports a correct divisor pair. Other points of that same product remain exposed until reported.

Removing the vertex $P$ leaves its neighboring vertices extreme. Let $u,v$ be the primitive edge directions from $P$ to its two neighbors, using $(0,1)$ at a vertical end. The new boundary is obtained by inserting the extreme points of the convex hull of nonzero lattice points in the cone spanned by $u,v$, translated by $P$. These points lie in the triangle with vertices $P,P+u,P+v$, which is contained in the old hull. The cone supplies the lower bounds, and the triangle realizes the new chain inside the old hull. All lattice points of the current polygon remain eligible because each removed point was extreme and lies outside the next polygon. This proves the update even when several adjacent vertices have equal product. The heap keeps surviving neighbors and inserts each new vertex once. The cone formula for removing a lattice polygon vertex is an established result of Bohnert and Springer. [Vertex removal and Hilbert bases](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17244)

For the update, put the rays in positive determinant order. Starting at $u$, choose its determinant-one neighbor $w$ with $0\le\det(w,v)<\det(u,v)$ by Bézout. At successive basis vectors $r,s$, set $D=\det(r,v)>E=\det(s,v)>0$. The next vector is $\lceil D/E\rceil s-r$. This keeps the basis unimodular and reduces the determinant to $\lceil D/E\rceil E-D$. These adjacent unimodular cones give the punctured-cone boundary, including its collinear lattice points. A quotient of two gives another point on the same straight segment. With $d=D-E$, jump over $\lfloor E/d\rfloor$ such steps at once along $s-r$, leaving remainder $E\bmod d$. For a quotient at least three, $E<D/2$. For a compressed run of twos the remainder is less than $E/2$, apart from the terminal zero. Thus the code produces only $O(b)$ extreme points per update and spends $O(b^3)$ bit operations including heap repairs. It never materializes a long collinear Hilbert basis.

### Interval size and prior bounds

On a balanced column interval $A,B\asymp\sqrt X$ of width $W=B-A$, the initial vertex count satisfies $K=O(1+W^{1/3}+WX^{-1/6})$. Close the lower chain by the chord joining its rounded endpoints. The rounded chord lies at most one above the true chord, which exceeds $X/x$ by $X(x-A)(B-x)/(ABx)$. Integrating gives area $O(W+W^3/\sqrt X)$. Andrews' lattice polygon bound gives $K=O(1+\mathrm{area}^{1/3})$ for a chain enclosing positive area. A collinear chain has only its endpoints. The actual $K$ can be much smaller. [Andrews, lattice-volume bound](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-lower-bound-for-the-volume-of-strictly-convex-bodies-with-many-/)

A second bound is stronger for short balanced intervals,
$$
K=O\!\left((1+WX^{-1/6})\log(2X)\right).
$$
Partition the columns into blocks of width $w\le cX^{1/6}$, with $c>0$ sufficiently small for the balance constants. In each block the lattice points above the hyperbola but below its exact secant lie in a parallelogram between that line and its vertical translate by $O(w^2/\sqrt X)$. Its area is $O(w^3/\sqrt X)<1/2$. Three noncollinear lattice points span a triangle of area at least $1/2$, so these exceptional points contribute at most two endpoints to the local integer hull. The portion on or above the secant is the integer hull of a rational polyhedron with three inequalities of $O(\log(2X))$ bits. The planar bound of Cook, Hartmann, Kannan and McDiarmid gives $O(\log(2X))$ vertices there. Every global extreme point remains extreme on a block containing it. This proves the bound on summing over blocks. In particular, initialization costs only polynomially many bit operations for any balanced column interval of width at most $X^{1/6}$, irrespective of rounding at its endpoints. [Cook et al., On integer points in polyhedra](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01191202)

For the full column interval $K=O(X^{1/3}\log(2X))$ by the known global hull bound, with at most one extra endpoint on the final horizontal ray. The exact output size is $\sum_{X\le n\le Z}\tau(n)$. For $X\le Z\le2X$, Shiu's bound for $\tau$ in intervals of length at least $X^{1/3}$, enlarging a shorter interval if necessary, gives $O((Z-X+X^{1/3}+1)\log(2X))$ outputs. The full sieve consequently has bit cost $\widetilde O(X^{1/3}+Z-X+1)$. Helfgott's published interval sieve already factors every integer of $[n-\Delta,n+\Delta]$ for $\Delta\ge n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3}$ in $O(\Delta\log n)$ word operations. The contribution here is the dynamic, column-restricted divisor stream with work controlled by initial vertices and reported pairs. The global single-threshold hull method already appears in the repository. [Shiu](https://doi.org/10.1515/CRLL.1980.313.161) · [Helfgott, Main Theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.09130#page=3) · [Prior repository analysis](../../notes/analytic_results.md)

### Reproducible checks

Independent enumeration verified 1,804 initial clipped hulls, 962 punctured cones, and all 2,811 events in 301 small windows. On the 512 consecutive integers from 10,000,123 through 10,000,634, full initialization took 1,273 vertices and produced all 8,670 divisor pairs. Restricting columns to $[2200,4300]$ took 92 initial vertices and reported the 309 pairs in that band. Both lists agreed with direct divisor enumeration.

With $s=2^{160}$, the control over $2^{79}+1$ columns centered at $s$ reports exactly three events from $s^2-1$ through $s^2+1$. The two orientations of $(s-1,s+1)$ occur at the first level and $(s,s)$ at the second. A separate query certifies zero events on the same columns over all $2^{159}$ levels from $s^2+1$ through $s^2+s/2$. This tests how work scales when both index intervals are enormous and the hull is small. [Runnable checks](check_hyperbola_hull_sieve.py)
