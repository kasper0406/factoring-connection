# Testing a large set of seeds with one resultant

At $N=31{,}439$ the additive gcd stays constant along every orbit $x\mapsto x^N$. A translated set can reach a different class. Starting at 3, four public powering steps give $t=10994$. It satisfies
$$\Psi_{15}(t)=t^4-t^3-4t^2+4t+1=0\bmod N.$$
Here $\Psi_{15}$ is the minimal polynomial of $\zeta_{15}+\zeta_{15}^{-1}$. For $D_4(t)=(t^2-2)^2-2$, the translated seed $3+D_4(t)=16683\bmod N$ gives $\gcd(16683^2+16683+1,N)=211$. The trace order and translate were selected using the known factors. Computing the seed and verifying the gcd use only $N$.

There is a precise scalar interpretation on this example. Both primes are coprime to 15 and are $-1$ and $+1\bmod 15$, respectively. Consequently Frobenius fixes all four primitive traces at each prime. The four translated seeds are $10997,16521,16683,18690$. With $H(X)=X(X+1)(X^2+X+1)$ the single resultant $\operatorname{Res}(H(X),\Psi_{15}(X-3))$ is $12449\bmod N$ and gives the same factor. For these fixed parameters the resultant is one integer independent of $N$. The example shows that an explicit change of seed can leave a failed orbit. It supplies no rule to pick these parameters on an arbitrary input. ## A batch whose cost is polynomial in the exponent's bit length

Let $H$ be a public monic polynomial of degree $D\ge1$. Supply $m\ge1$, a unit $u\bmod N$ and a translate $c$. Compute
$$R_c=\operatorname{Res}_X\bigl(H(X),(X-c)^m-u\bigr)\bmod N.$$
In $A=(\mathbb Z/N)[X]/H$, compute $b=(X-c)^m-u$ by binary powering, then take the determinant of multiplication by $b$ on $A$. This norm gives the stated resultant because $H$ is monic. The bit cost is polynomial in $D,\log N,\log m$. At a hidden prime $r$, the resultant vanishes when the two polynomials share a root over the algebraic closure of $\mathbb F_r$.

This last distinction changes how to interpret a hit. At $N=35$, $H=X^2+1$, $m=4$, $c=1$ and $u=3$, the resultant is $14\bmod 35$, exposing 7. The common root lies in $\mathbb F_{49}$ and $X^2+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_7$. The diagnostic checks 15,300 translations against independent polynomial gcds over the prime fields. It also verifies a 512-bit exponent against direct powering at three known integer roots. [Implementation and checks](check_translated_batch.py)

**Uniform translations.** Fix distinct odd primes $N=pq$. Let $H,m,u$ depend arbitrarily on $N$, but fix them before the fresh uniform draw of $c\bmod N$. At each hidden prime $r$,
$$\Pr(r\mid R_c)\le \frac{D\,\gcd(m,r-1)}{r}.$$
To prove this, take any irreducible factor $h$ of $H\bmod r$, with degree $d$ and root $\alpha$. If $(\alpha-c)^m=u$, taking the field norm gives $\big((-1)^d h(c)\big)^m=u^d$ in $\mathbb F_r$. There are at most $\gcd(m,r-1)$ possible nonzero norm values and at most $d$ shifts for each. Sum over distinct factors of $H$, whose degrees total at most $D$. Repeated roots and irreducible factors of higher degree are both covered.

A proper gcd with probability at least $\delta$ therefore implies $(r-1)/\gcd(m,r-1)\le 2D/\delta$ for at least one prime. The public integer $E=m\,\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,\lceil2D/\delta\rceil)$ is a multiple of that prime’s group order. From a random unit compute gcds at the odd part of $E$ and at its successive doublings through $E$. If the other order is uncovered the endpoint splits with probability at least $1/2$. If it is covered as well, the independent local orders have different 2-adic valuations with probability at least $1/2$. This gives a separate factoring algorithm from the same supplied exponent. The earlier result treated a quadratic with supplied scalar roots and exponent $N-1$. [Quadratic precedent](bounded-quadratic-roots.md#a-public-power-denominator) · [Exponent completion and scalar splitting](bounded-quadratic-roots.md#completing-an-exponent-that-covers-one-hidden-prime)

This bound concerns fresh uniform shifts. A particular $c$ chosen using the arithmetic of $N$ may hit without a large local kernel. A different aggregation is to take all preimages $f^{\circ k}(X)=a$ of a scalar under a fixed monic map. The same small-algebra trick evaluates the resultant against $H$ by iterating $f$ at the roots of $H$. For uniform $a$ there are at most $D$ possible scalar target values at each hidden prime. Comparing two whole preimage trees of one map has an exact formula. For $k\ge j$ the resultant of $f^{\circ j}(X)-b$ and $f^{\circ k}(X)-a$ equals $(f^{\circ(k-j)}(b)-a)^{(\deg f)^j}$, by multiplying over the first polynomial’s roots, with multiplicity.

## Factoring with an exponent for a norm-one group

**Historical precedent.** Algorithm B of Bach, Miller and Shallit (1986) already draws a random monic polynomial, raises a random quotient element to a supplied exponent and uses its nonconstant coefficients to split the modulus. In their application the exponent is $\sigma(N)$, a multiple of $1+r+\cdots+r^a$ when $r^a$ exactly divides $N$. This is the extraction mechanism used below. The translated-batch bounds specify when the present probes would supply such an exponent. The repository had already identified the cyclotomic factoring connection. [Algorithm B, steps B3–B7 and Lemmas E–G](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/BMS86.pdf#page=6) · [Earlier repository discussion](../../notes/trackBD_analysis.md) · [Bach and Shallit on cyclotomic factoring](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/1989-52-185/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1.pdf)

Suppose a public positive integer $E$ and degree $d\ge2$ satisfy
$$S_d(r)=\frac{r^d-1}{r-1}\ \mid\ E$$
for at least one hidden prime $r$ of the odd semiprime $N$. This suffices to find a factor in randomized time polynomial in $d$, $\log E$ and $\log N$, even when $d$ and both norm-one group orders are odd. The promise concerns just one of the two primes. The algorithm does not need to know which. A missing cofactor $S_d(r)/\gcd(S_d(r),m)\le B$ is enough by taking $E=m\,\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,B)$.

Run the scalar exponent test above as well as the following trials. Write $s$ for the other prime. When $s-1\mid E$, the scalar test already suffices. Otherwise draw a uniform monic polynomial $f$ of degree $d$ modulo $N$ and a uniform element $y$ of the quotient algebra. Compute just $v=y^E\bmod(f,N)$ and take the gcd of $N$ with **all nonconstant coefficients** of $v$. If $d$ is unknown within a stated polynomial range, cycle through one trial for each candidate degree.

At $r$, a random $f$ is irreducible with probability at least $1/(2d)$ by counting the elements of degree $d$ in $\mathbb F_{r^d}$. Then $y^{S_d(r)}$ is the field norm, so $v$ is scalar for every $y$, including zero. At $s$, the probability that $f$ is squarefree with at least one linear factor is at least $1/2-1/s$. Indeed the number $Z$ of distinct scalar roots of a random monic has moments $\mathbb E Z=1$ and $\mathbb E Z^2=2-1/s$ by evaluating at one or two distinct points. Also the fraction of monic polynomials of degree $d\ge2$ that are not squarefree is $1/s$. On the desired event the algebra has the form $\mathbb F_s\times C$ with $C$ of positive dimension. Fixing the component in $C$ leaves at most one candidate scalar value for $v$. The independent uniform linear coordinate $a$ has probability at most $\max(1,\gcd(E,s-1))/s\le1/2$ of attaining that value after powering. CRT makes the two prime-side events independent, giving $\Omega(1/d)$ proper-split probability per trial. This calculation applies when both norm-one orders are odd as well.

For example, take $N=1591=37\cdot43$ and the supplied odd exponent $E=887817$, a multiple of *both* cubic norm-one orders. The polynomial $X^3+5X+1$ is irreducible modulo 37 and has the linear root 3 modulo 43. With $y=X+1$, the coefficient gcd returns 37. The coefficient vector of $y^E$ is $(1142,592,555)\bmod1591$, and $\gcd(1591,592,555)=37$. The implementation also verified proper factors on 15 supplied-exponent semiprime cases in degrees 2 through 6, on a product of three primes and on an input with a repeated prime. These tests constructed the exponents using known factors.

The same two-prime proof works for **any odd $N$ with at least two distinct prime divisors**, including repeated prime factors. Vanishing at $r$ and survival at one other prime $s$ suffice for a nontrivial proper gcd, irrespective of multiplicities. If the scalar fallback covers $s$, pair it with any other prime in the scalar argument. CRT gives independent draws at the selected primes. Thus a polynomial-time constructor producing a polynomial-bit exponent with a polynomially bounded missing cofactor for one hidden norm-one group on **each** such input would factor all integers in randomized polynomial time. Strip powers of 2, handle prime powers by integer roots and primality testing, and invoke the constructor anew on composite pieces. Conversely, the factors supply $d=2$ and $E=r+1$ immediately.

The earlier cubic construction needed exactly one of two irreducible local factors to have covered image order in that test. Here different factorization types of a newly drawn polynomial make one-sided coverage sufficient for all degrees. The code takes $E$ as input. Obtaining an appropriate $E$ from an unfactored $N$ is the remaining factoring problem in this construction. ## A uniform shift between conjugates in an extension field

Fix a hidden prime $r\mid N$, a degree-$d$ irreducible factor with root $\alpha$ in $E=\mathbb F_{r^d}$, and $d\ge2$. Suppose the proposed conjugation on this component is $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{r^h}$ with $\gcd(h,d)=1$. For uniform $c\in\mathbb F_r$, set
$$
z(c)=\frac{c-\alpha}{c-\alpha^{r^h}}.
$$
Both factors are nonzero for every $c$. This local description applies when the algorithm can implement the conjugation, for example through a specified algebra automorphism. Generic powering by the input $N$ does not supply the hidden Frobenius.

Write $T=\ker(\operatorname{Norm}_{E/\mathbb F_r})$, of order $t=(r^d-1)/(r-1)$. The map $\pi(y)=y^{1-r^h}$ sends $E^\times$ onto $T$. Its kernel has size $\gcd(r^d-1,r^h-1)=r-1$, and its image has norm one. Further, $z(c)=\pi(c-\alpha)$. Thus each nontrivial multiplicative character $\chi$ of $T$ pulls back to a nontrivial character of $E^\times$. Since $\mathbb F_r(\alpha)=E$, Katz's Theorem 1 gives
$$
\left|\sum_{c\in\mathbb F_r}\chi(z(c))\right|\le(d-1)\sqrt r .
$$
The theorem includes characters trivial on $\mathbb F_r^\times$. [N. M. Katz, *An estimate for character sums*, JAMS 2 (1989), Theorem 1, p. 197](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~nmk/old/estcharsums.pdf#page=2)

Fix $m>0$ and a target $u\in E^\times$ independent of $c$. If $u\notin T^m$, the hit probability is zero. Otherwise the solutions to $z^m=u$ form one coset of $T[m]$ in $T$, of index $I=t/\gcd(t,m)$. Expand its indicator in the $I$ quotient characters. The principal term is $1/I$. The other $I-1$ terms have coefficients of modulus $1/I$, so
$$
\left|\Pr_c[z(c)^m=u]-\frac1I\right|
\le\left(1-\frac1I\right)\frac{d-1}{\sqrt r}.
$$

For a resultant whose hits all come from components of the stated kind, let $D$ bound the sum of their degrees at each prime of $N=pq$, $p<q$. Summing the upper bounds over the two primes costs at most $2D/\sqrt p$ in error and at most $2D$ reciprocal-index terms. A proper-split probability at least $\delta$ and $p\ge(4D/\delta)^2$ therefore force $I\le4D/\delta$ on at least one component. For smaller $p$ trial division through that threshold suffices. With polynomial $D$, inverse-polynomial $\delta$ in the input bit length, and polynomial-bit $m$, the public exponent $m\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,\lceil4D/\delta\rceil)$ has polynomial bit length and is divisible by the full norm-one order of that component. The absolute norm-one sampler can alternate trials over all $2\le d\le D$. Constant ratios, including linear factors, require separate treatment.

When $1<e=\gcd(h,d)<d$, the same character argument uses the relative norm kernel of order $(r^d-1)/(r^e-1)$. A supplied multiple of that order can also be used in a factoring algorithm. Draw a monic degree-$e$ polynomial to form a coefficient algebra $B$, and a new monic polynomial of degree $d/e$ over $B$ to form $A$. Take a uniform $y\in A$ and test the gcd of $N$ with all coefficients of $y^E$ outside the public subalgebra $B$. At $r$ both stages are irreducible with joint probability $\Omega(1/d)$, giving the finite field tower over which $y^E$ belongs to the intermediate field. At $s$ require a squarefree polynomial with a linear factor in the base stage, then the same event for the upper polynomial at that linear component. Each costs at most a constant factor in probability by the preceding root count. Within the resulting upper algebra over $\mathbb F_s$, the scalar-coordinate test succeeds with probability at least $1/2$ if $s-1\nmid E$. Membership in $B$ requires membership in $\mathbb F_s$ at this component. The scalar fallback handles $s-1\mid E$. When $h$ is divisible by $d$, the ratio is the constant 1. Algorithm C of the same 1986 paper already uses a two-level random algebra and the coefficient test in the upper variable. [Bach, Miller and Shallit, steps C2–C6](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/BMS86.pdf#page=9)

The relative order also contains a classical cyclotomic factor. For a prime $\ell$ with $v_\ell(d)>v_\ell(e)$, take $j=1+v_\ell(e)$. Then $\Phi_{\ell^j}(r)$ divides $(r^d-1)/(r^e-1)$ by cyclotomic factorization. Bach and Shallit recount the earlier factoring result for supplied multiples at prime-power indices in their introduction. [Historical reference, p. 202](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/1989-52-185/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1/S0025-5718-1989-0947467-1.pdf#page=2)

The absolute algorithm is implemented for arbitrary degree. A separate small check illustrates the relative calculation. For $N=35$ use $B$ generated by $U$ with $U^2=2$ and $A$ generated by $V$ with $V^2=U$. This is the quartic quotient $V^4=2$ with $B$ spanned by $1,V^2$. For the supplied $E=5^2+1=26$, the element $(1+V)^E$ has coefficients $(11,0,9,25)\bmod35$. Its two odd-power coefficients give $\gcd(35,0,25)=5$.

## Exponents given by a bounded-degree polynomial in the input

Choose distinct primes $r,s$ uniformly without replacement from $[R,2R]$ and set $N=rs$. Fix a polynomial-size menu of primitive integer polynomials $P$ before drawing either prime. Here primitive means that all coefficients together have gcd 1. Degrees satisfy $1\le\deg P\le k$ for a fixed $k$. Take the positive exponent $m=|P(N)|$, omitting zero. Given any fixed polynomial bounds in $\log R$ on the missing cofactor $B$ and on the degrees $d$, the probability of covering a scalar or norm-one order up to cofactor $B$ on either side is at most
$$R^{-2/[k(k+1)]+o(1)}.$$
The test may choose among all the listed $P$ and all the allowed $d$ using $N$. The assertion concerns the order-coverage promise. It gives no upper bound on incidental success of the public splitter on inputs outside that promise.

Fix $r$. Take $S=r-1$ for a scalar group, $S=S_d(r)$ for $d\ge2$, or $S=(r^d-1)/(r^e-1)$ for a proper divisor $e$ of $d$. All three choices satisfy $S\ge r-1$ and $r\nmid S$. The promise $S/\gcd(S,m)\le B$ implies that some $t\le B$ divides $S$ and $L=S/t$ divides $P(rs)$. We have $L\ge(R-1)/B$ and $r$ is coprime to $L$. Write $j=\deg P$ and $K=j(j+1)/2$. Take $j+1$ distinct candidate partner integers $s_i$ at which $P(rs_i)\equiv0\pmod L$. Their Vandermonde matrix on powers $0,\ldots,j$ kills the coefficient vector of $P(rX)$ modulo $L$. Multiplying by the adjugate shows that its determinant times each coefficient vanishes modulo $L$. These coefficients generate the unit ideal modulo $L$ because $P$ is primitive and $r$ is invertible. Therefore $L$ divides the nonzero integer $\prod_{i<l}(s_l-s_i)$.

An interval of length $(L/2)^{1/K}$ cannot hold $j+1$ such partners. Covering the dyadic interval and dividing by the number of prime partners gives probability $O_k(\log R)(R^{-1}+L^{-1/K})$ per $L$. Sum over $t$, both sides, the degree choices and the exponent menu. All these extra counts are polynomial in $\log R$. This proves the displayed estimate uniformly in the fixed prime $r$ before symmetrizing. It also covers repeated roots of $P$ modulo the prime powers in $L$. A genuinely input-dependent construction of $m$ outside a shared polynomial menu remains open.
