# Which dyadic binomial questions does the product already answer?

For distinct odd primes $p<q<2p$, write $N=pq$. The zero test attached to a mask $M$ is positive precisely when both $p$ and $q$ have at least one of its selected binary digits set. Positions begin at 1 here, since every candidate is odd. We can decide in polynomial bit time whether an answer follows from the product modulo a power of two or from the balanced size range alone. On a 12-input cohort these tests resolved 28 of 667 singleton questions and 23 of 23,525 pair questions. Every certified answer agreed with the factors. ## Exact product residues

Let $j$ be the largest selected position and $P=2^{j+1}$. Run over odd $a\bmod P$ and put $b=N/a\bmod P$. The choice $a=1$ always gives a negative answer. All masks with at least two selected bits also admit a positive answer. Indeed the subset $S$ of odd units with at least one selected bit has density at least $3/4$. The map $a\mapsto N/a$ permutes the units, so the intersection of $S$ with its inverse image has density at least $1/2$.

The following are all the cases in which a nonempty singleton mask has a forced answer. Each answer in the table is negative.

| Position $j$ | Modulus $2^{j+1}$ | Product residues forcing no |
|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 4 | 3 |
| 2 | 8 | 5, 7 |
| 3 | 16 | 11, 13 |
| 4 | 32 | 19, 29 |

For the proof, let $S$ be the upper half of the odd residues. If a positive answer is impossible, inversion followed by multiplication by $N$ must exchange the two halves, since they have equal size. Set $H=2^{\lceil(j+1)/2\rceil}$, $L=P/H$. For fixed odd $0<u<H$ put $a=u+Hz$. Because $H^2$ is divisible by $P$, the high digit $\lfloor b/H\rfloor\bmod L$ is affine in $z$, with slope $-N/u^2\bmod L$. The affine map must exchange the contiguous halves of $\mathbf Z/L$. Its slope must be $\pm1$ when $L\ge4$. Indeed a half interval intersects its unit translate in $L/2-1$ points, and only translates by $\pm1$ have this overlap. Applying the map to this intersection gives the assertion. Taking $u=1,3$ now gives $9\equiv\pm1\bmod L$, impossible when $L\ge16$. This handles $j\ge7$. Exhaustion for $1\le j\le6$ gives the table, including the two remaining empty rows. The code can also construct a positive witness at arbitrarily large positions. It counts the upper-half intersections on these two affine fibers using Euclidean floor sums and locates the first hit by binary search. A solution at one period lifts to every power-of-two refinement with the same prescribed product by choosing a lift of $a$ and computing its inverse partner.

Adding $P/2$ to $a$ toggles both top bits. Replacing $N$ by $-N$ toggles only the top bit of $b$, since $b$ is odd. Thus a singleton in the table always has opposite bits, and a singleton whose *negative* product residue is in the table always has equal bits. Outside these cases all four ordered bit pairs occur. Averaging over the sixteen odd product residues modulo 32 gives exactly $11/8$ forced singleton zero tests per input, across all bit positions.

There is also an exact prediction statement in the uniform residue model. Given the whole product residue, a singleton is positive with conditional probability at most $1/2$, while a pair is positive with probability at least $1/2$. The optimal constant decisions, no and yes respectively, therefore remain optimal after observing that residue. For independent uniform odd inputs their success probabilities are $3/4$ and $9/16$. These apply in the limit for independent distinct primes in $[R,2R]$ at any fixed period, by the prime number theorem in progressions. Growing periods and algorithms using the full integer $N$ are outside this prediction statement. [Earlier product-fiber model](../../notes/trackE_analysis.md)

## The real balanced hyperbola

Take real $u\in[\sqrt{N/2},\sqrt N]$ and $v=N/u$. Evaluate the bits of $\lfloor u\rfloor,\lfloor v\rfloor$. Their possible individual values fill all integers from $\lfloor\sqrt{N/2}\rfloor$ through $\lfloor\sqrt{2N}\rfloor$. A negative answer exists precisely when that interval contains some word avoiding $M$. Starting at the lower endpoint, skip past the most significant conflicting bit and clear its suffix. Every skipped value has a conflict, and each further conflict must occur higher in the word.

For a positive witness, if a selected bit has period $P_0\le\sqrt N/8$, the $u$ range contains a complete upper-half interval of length $P_0/2$. The image of its interior under $u\mapsto N/u$ has length strictly exceeding $P_0/2$. It therefore meets a bit-one interval for $v$. Otherwise all effective periods are comparable to $\sqrt N$, so only a constant number of bit-change boundaries meet the balanced range. On the axis $z=u^2$ those boundaries and both endpoints are rational. Testing each boundary and one sample between successive boundaries handles an isolated true answer when two bit changes coincide. The calculation is exact even with a large mask. A forced answer on this continuous relaxation holds on actual balanced factors. ### Exactly which pairs have a forced positive answer?

Put $t=\sqrt{N/2}$, let $A$ be the smallest power of two above $t$, and set $D=A-\lfloor t\rfloor$. Our odd semiprime promise implies $A/2<t<A$. Some coordinate attains each floor value from $\lfloor t\rfloor$ through $\lfloor 2t\rfloor$, including $A$. Consequently every forced positive mask must contain $A$. No singleton is forced positive, because $\lfloor t\rfloor<A$. A two-bit mask is forced positive **exactly** when its weights are $A$ and $w$ with $D\le w<A$. All floor values at or above $A$ have bit $A$ throughout this range. Below $A$, the contiguous upper tail $[\lfloor t\rfloor,A-1]$ has bit $w$ at every value precisely when it lies inside $[A-w,A-1]$.

Conversely, a negative answer can be forced by the real test only when every selected weight exceeds $\sqrt N/16$, by the full-period argument. There are at most five such active weights below $\sqrt{2N}$, so at most five singleton and ten pair candidates for forced negative answers. The local test can add at most four more forced negative singletons. The intersection of the two screens therefore decides $O(1+\log(A/D))$ questions among **all** active singletons and pairs.

For two distinct primes drawn uniformly in $[R,2R]$ this last count is $O(1)$ in expectation. There are only a bounded number of possible powers $A$ across the sample. The event $D\le R2^{-k}$ requires, for fixed $p$ and some such $A$, that $q=2t^2/p$ is within $O(R2^{-k})$ of $2A^2/p$. For $0\le k\le (\log_2 R)/2$, Brun–Titchmarsh bounds its probability by $O(2^{-k})$ after division by the number of prime pairs. The remaining $O(\log R)$ scales each have probability $O(R^{-1/2})$ by monotonicity. Summing the tails gives the expectation. This counts exact certificates in the two specified relaxations.

## Combining odd residues, integer coordinates and the floor quotient

There is a stronger conclusion when $\sqrt N\ge64P^3$. Every locally compatible odd pair $a,b\bmod P$ can be realized by positive integers $x\le y\le2x$ with $x\equiv a$, $y\equiv b$, and **$y=\lfloor N/x\rfloor$**. Let $U=\sqrt N$ and take the least integer $h\ge2U$ congruent to $a+b\bmod P$. Write $\delta=\sqrt{h^2-4N}$ and $t=(h-\delta)/2$. Choose the largest $x\le t$ in class $a$, and set $y=h-x$. Then
$$
0\le N-xy=(t-x)(\delta+t-x)<3P\sqrt{PU}+P^2.
$$
Here $h-2U<P$ and $\delta<3\sqrt{PU}$. Also $x>U-\frac32\sqrt{PU}-P$. Since $U\ge64P^3$ and $P\ge1$, these inequalities imply $N-xy<x$ and $h\le3x$. For the balance inequality one may use $\sqrt{PU}\le U/8$ and $P\le U/64$, giving $x>51U/64$ and $h<129U/64$. Congruence gives $P\mid N-xy$. The construction uses only integer square roots and division. The remainder can be nonzero. Thus through this range even the integer floor-quotient relaxation, including prescribed odd residues, has exactly the same possible answers as the full local table. The actual divisor equation still requires zero remainder.

The checker compared every odd product residue and every singleton/pair whose highest bit is 1 through 9, totaling 8,194 mask/product tests. It also constructed 100 simultaneous lifts through position 256. The independent archimedean checker compared 3,098 smaller masks against exact floor cells and tested 480 further lifts. On 229 balanced prime pairs with both primes between 17 and 149, the intersection screen certified 497 of 1,432 singleton answers and 404 of 3,824 pair answers. For twelve held-out prime pairs with 24, 48 or 96 bits per prime, 28 singleton and 23 pair answers were certified, including 20 positive pair answers. Positions larger than both possible factors were excluded in these totals. At a separate 255-bit input, the continuous test certified two of 8,001 pair answers. ## Even after the earlier greedy answers have been supplied

One may restrict the smaller real factor to any known interval $[L,H]$ inside the balanced range. A negative answer exists when a word avoiding the selection occurs in $[L,H]$ or $[N/H,N/L]$ after taking floors. A selected bit of period $P_0$ guarantees a positive answer whenever $H-L\ge2P_0$, by the same full-interval argument. Otherwise every selected bit has width greater than $(H-L)/4$. Since the reciprocal interval has length at most $2(H-L)$, each selected bit supplies only $O(1)$ rational cutpoints in $z=u^2$. The implementation treats empty and point intervals exactly.

For the greedy largest-prime reduction on balanced $N=pq$, suppose that *all previous answers were supplied correctly*. Above bit zero, the smaller mask is $p$. At bit $j$, with $w=2^j$, the known prefix places $p$ in an aligned interval $[a,a+2w-1]$. Every earlier selected position is zero throughout this interval. With $U=\sqrt N$, set
$$
\delta=\min(p-U/\sqrt2,U-p)\ \ge\ \tfrac14\min(2p-q,q-p)>0.
$$
If $2w\le\delta/2$, the entire prefix interval lies in the balanced range and below $U-\delta/2$. Its lower half gives a negative answer. Its upper half, with width $w-1$, has bit $j$ set. There $|dv/du|=N/u^2\ge 1+\delta/U$. If $w>1+U/\delta$, the reciprocal image therefore has length greater than $w$ and attains bit $j$ set, giving a positive answer. All bits between these two cutoffs are ambiguous even with the known prefix. Counting powers of two at the two ends shows that at most $O(1+\log(U/\delta))$ of the active greedy questions can be certified by this interval calculation.

For two distinct primes drawn uniformly from $[R,2R]$ and then sorted, its expected number of certified answers is **$O(1)$**. Indeed, for $0\le k\le(\log_2 R)/2$, the distance $\min(2p-q,q-p)$ can be at most $h=R/2^k$ only if $q$ lies in one of two intervals of length $h$ for the given $p$. Brun–Titchmarsh bounds each count by $O(h/\log h)$. Multiplying by $O(R/\log R)$ choices of $p$ and dividing by $\asymp R^2/\log^2R$ pairs gives probability $O(2^{-k})$. Sum these tails. For larger $k$ the total contribution is $O(R^{-1/2}\log R)$ by monotonicity and the positive integer gap bound. This counts deductions from the one-dimensional real interval and its reciprocal, with earlier bit answers as side information. It gives no lower bound for other algorithms using the integer $N$. [Montgomery and Vaughan, *The large sieve*](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579300004708)
