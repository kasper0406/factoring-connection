# Factoring a smaller word of the product to recover factor suffixes

Let $N=pq$ with distinct odd primes $p<q<2p$. For a public power $m$ of two, let $[z]_m$ be its centered residue in $[-m/2,m/2)$. Write $x=[p]_m$, $y=[q]_m$ and $r=[N]_m$. Their exact integer product is $xy=r+jm$ for an unknown carry $j$. If $|j|\le B$, the factors of just $2B+1$ public integers of size $O((B+1)m)$ give a complete list containing the true suffix. Testing that list uses the original $N$. An additional near-square condition on the smaller integer gives a polynomial-bit-time factorization for a particular family even when ordinary Fermat search on $N$ is infeasible. ## Exact list and deterministic cost

For each $D=r+jm$, $-B\le j\le B$, take the divisors $h>0$ of $|D|$ such that $h,|D|/h<m/2$ and include $\pm h\bmod m$. An odd residue $a$ belongs to the resulting list exactly when the product of its centered value and that of $Na^{-1}\bmod m$ has an allowed carry. Indeed the inverse partner is unique in the centered interval. Either factor can serve as $a$. This is the point at which factoring the smaller integer gives information about an actual factor residue. A locally admissible product need not arise from any divisor of $N$.

For polylogarithmic $B$, the list has $m^{o(1)}$ entries using the standard divisor bound at $m=N^{\Theta(1)}$. Deterministic integer factorization obtains the smaller factorizations in $m^{1/5+o(1)}$ total bit operations. The balanced residue-list algorithm needs at most $(1+N^{1/4}/m)m^{o(1)}$ additional bit operations. Thus choosing $m\asymp N^{5/24}$ gives a deterministic **$N^{1/24+o(1)}$** algorithm under the stated carry promise. The reference implementation uses SymPy to enumerate the auxiliary divisors. The one-fifth exponent refers to the published deterministic algorithm. [Harvey and Hittmeir](https://doi.org/10.1090/mcom/3708) · [Residue-list completion](hh-residue-lists.md)

For independent uniform odd $x,y$ modulo $m$, a short carry requires $|xy|\le(B+1/2)m$. For each nonzero $x$ one can count at most $O(1+\min(m,(B+1)m/|x|))$ choices of $y$. Summing gives probability $O((B+1)\log m/m)$. For distinct primes in $[R,2R]$, Brun–Titchmarsh and the prime number theorem give an $O(1/m)$ upper bound on the probability of each odd residue when $m\le R^{1-\epsilon}$ with fixed $\epsilon>0$. The same upper bound for the promise follows, including the input-dependent choice of $m$ from bit length since only constantly many values occur on the dyadic prime cohort. At the chosen scale, coverage is at most $N^{-5/24+o(1)}$. An incidental split from a list that missed the true suffix lies outside this count.

## A polynomial reader requiring only square tests on the smaller integers

Take $m\asymp N^{1/4}$ with a sufficient constant for one affine window per suffix. For each $D$, test whether $t^2-|D|$ is square in the first $L$ integer centers $t\ge\sqrt{|D|}$. Each square yields the pair of absolute factor residues $t\pm\sqrt{t^2-|D|}$. The true pair is found whenever $|j|\le B$ and
$$
\frac{|x|+|y|}{2}-\lceil\sqrt{|xy|}\rceil<L.
$$
The left side is an integer; it is the zero-indexed position of this pair in Fermat's search on $|D|$. A sufficient condition is $(\sqrt{|x|}-\sqrt{|y|})^2<2L$. After adding both signs there are at most $4(2B+1)L$ residue classes, each with polynomial completion cost. This gives an input-only polynomial-time split for polynomial $B,L$ in $\log N$. The implementation uses 12 extra bits for $m$ to reduce lattice dimensions on the finite checks.

This promise permits both absolute residues of order $N^{1/8}$ and differences of order $N^{1/16}$ without requiring $p\equiv\pm q$ modulo a long power of two. It occurs less often than just the short carry. Put $H=(B+1/2)m$. For $u=\min(|x|,|y|)$ and $v=\max(|x|,|y|)$ we have $u\le\sqrt H$ and $(\sqrt v-\sqrt u)^2<2L$ when the true center is reached, since the midpoint is integral. Hence $v-u=O(\sqrt{Lu}+L)$. The number of qualifying ordered signed word pairs is $O(\sqrt L H^{3/4}+L H^{1/2})$. The same progression bound implies probability at most $N^{-5/16+o(1)}$ for polynomial budgets on the balanced prime sample.

The tests compared both word lists against every odd product residue through $m=512$, totaling 1,530 modulus/residue/budget cases. Full divisor lists at carries $-1,0,1$ split three planted products of 20-, 28- and 36-bit primes using the Harvey–Hittmeir collision stage. The true residue appeared in 31, 2 and 0 of 100 unplanted controls at prime sizes 16, 32 and 48. The second method split four further planted inputs with 48-, 64-, 96- and 128-bit primes using one center per auxiliary word and one affine window each. On the largest example the suffixes differed by 207,242 and neither $p-q$ nor $p+q$ was divisible by 16. Direct signed Fermat search on $N$ required more than $4\cdot10^{36}$ centers per progression. These are input-only recoveries on constructed inputs. [Direct signed Fermat comparison](cofactor-sum-lifts.md)

## Relation to earlier attempts and a limit on algebraic descent

Previous binary-word tests used a word's prime divisors to supply factors of $p^2-1$ or $q^2-1$. Here a possibly composite divisor of a smaller word supplies $p$ or $q$ **modulo $m$**. Carry corrections and polynomial factorization in the radix were also tested previously. The current square search factors the constant product of two low words alone and uses the affine reader on $N$. [Earlier binary-word tests](digit-order-support.md) · [Earlier carry-polynomial construction](carry-polynomial.md)

Simply substituting $p=a+mP$, $q=b+mQ$ gives $(N-ab)/m=mPQ+bP+aQ$, which still contains the two unknown linear terms. There is an algebraic reason for this behavior. Let $R$ be the rationals with denominators coprime to $N$. On the curve $uv=N$, any pair of polynomial expressions over $R$ with a nonzero constant integral product and nonconstant restriction has product divisible by $N$. In $\mathbf Q[u,u^{-1}]$ both restrictions must be Laurent monomials of opposite degrees, since their product is constant. Terms of degree $-s<0$ in the image of $R[u,v]/(uv-N)$ have coefficients divisible by $N^s$ in $R$, as $v^s=N^s/u^s$. Nonnegative-degree coefficients are in $R$. Thus $N^s$ divides the integral product for the two opposing degree-$s$ monomials. This includes fixed polynomial transformations after division by powers of the known dyadic $m$. Passing to an input-specific carry promise, as above, escapes this identity.
