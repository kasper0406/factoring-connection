# Comparing a four-square point with its twisted conjugate

Fix distinct odd primes $R\le p<q\le2R$ and put $N=pq$. Draw a uniform signed, ordered integer point $\alpha$ of squared norm $N$. Pairing it with $\bar\alpha V(\alpha)$ gives a public factor test. If the coordinates of $V$ are homogeneous polynomials of common degree $k$, a coefficient gcd checks whether the formula itself singles out a prime. Otherwise the probability that a sample produces a proper divisor is at most $(k+1)R^{-1/2+o(1)}$. For an independent twist chosen uniformly among residues with unit norm, the exact probability is $(p+q)/((p+1)(q+1))$. The point sampler takes expected polynomial time without the factors. [Sampler and proof](four-square-incidence.md)

## The integer readout

Write $\mathcal H$ for the Hurwitz order and $d$ for the common right gcd of $\alpha$ and $\bar\alpha V$, so $\mathcal H d=\mathcal H\alpha+\mathcal H\bar\alpha V$. Then

$$\operatorname{norm}d=\gcd(N,\langle V,\alpha\rangle),\qquad
\langle V,\alpha\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^3V_j\alpha_j.$$

At $r=p,q$ use $\mathcal H/r\mathcal H\simeq M_2(\mathbb F_r)$, where conjugation is the adjugate. The reduction $A$ of $\alpha$ has rank one since $r$ divides its norm exactly once. Put $B=\operatorname{adj}(A)$ and write $G$ for the reduction of the evaluated twist. The left ideal generated by $A$ and $BG$ is proper exactly when their rows together span dimension one. Equivalently, $BG$ kills $\ker A=\operatorname{im}B$, or $BGB=0$. Writing the rank-one matrix $B$ as $uv^T$ gives $BGB=\operatorname{tr}(GB)B$. Its trace coefficient is $2\langle V,\alpha\rangle$ modulo $r$. This holds for singular $G$ as well. The generated left ideal has generator $d$, and $\operatorname{norm}d$ divides $N$, so these two local tests give the integer equality.

## Uniform bound for any prechosen twist

Let $W$ be a subspace of codimension $c$ in $\mathbb F_r^4$. The probability that $\alpha\bmod r\in W$ is $\ll_\epsilon r^{-c/2+\epsilon}$ for $c=1,3$. To see this, let $\Lambda$ be its inverse image in $\mathbb Z^4$, of index $r^c$. Take a size-reduced LLL basis with parameter $3/4$ and Gram–Schmidt lengths $s_1,\ldots,s_4$. The LLL inequalities give $s_{i+1}\ge s_i/\sqrt2$. Since their product is $r^c$, they give $s_3s_4\gg r^{c/2}$. Also $s_1\ge1$ and $s_4\le r$. For the latter, project $r\mathbb Z^4\subseteq\Lambda$ orthogonally off the first three basis vectors. Some $r e_j$ has a nonzero projection of length at most $r$ in a one-dimensional lattice of spacing $s_4$. Consequently all $s_i$ lie between an absolute positive constant and $O(r)$.

Write $\alpha=\sum z_i b_i$ in this basis. Back-substitution on the sphere of radius $\sqrt N\asymp r$ gives $O(1+\sqrt N/s_4)$ choices for $z_4$, then $O(1+\sqrt N/s_3)$ for $z_3$. The product is $O(N/r^{c/2})$. The size-reduction inequalities $|\mu_{ij}|\le 1/2$ and the lower bounds for $s_i$ give $|z_i|\ll\sqrt N$ by the same back-substitution. With $z_3,z_4$ fixed, the remaining plane meets the sphere in an empty set, a point, or an ellipse. In the ellipse case the homogenized ternary quadratic is nonsingular, and its binary restriction at infinity is positive definite. Lemma 4.1 of Browning–Gorodnik at homogenizing coordinate $1$ therefore bounds the number of integer points by $O_\epsilon(N^\epsilon)$, uniformly in **all** conic coefficients. The primitivity condition in the lemma is automatic at coordinate $1$. Divide the resulting count by Jacobi's $8(p+1)(q+1)$ and rescale $\epsilon$. [Browning–Gorodnik, Lemma 4.1, p. 33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.06342#page=33)

For a fixed twist whose coordinate gcd is coprime to $N$, apply $c=1$ to the nonzero linear form $\langle V,\cdot\rangle\bmod r$ and sum over $p,q$. A menu of $K$ such twists fixed before sampling costs a factor of at most $K$ by the union bound, even when the choice from the menu uses the observed point.

For twists with integer height $H=\max_j|V_j|$, there is also a bound $\ll_\epsilon (1/\sqrt N+H/r)N^\epsilon$ at prime $r$. There are $O(1+H\sqrt N/r)$ possible levels $\langle V,x\rangle=jr$. Fix one coordinate other than a chosen nonzero pivot of $V$, then eliminate the pivot using the level equation. This leaves an affine plane parameterized by the other two coordinates. Clearing denominators and applying the same conic bound costs $O_\epsilon(N^\epsilon)$ per slice. The fixed coordinate has $O(\sqrt N)$ choices. In particular $H=R^{o(1)}$ gives success at most $R^{-1+o(1)}$.

## Homogeneous tests chosen from the sampled point

Let $F\in(\mathbb Z/N)[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]$ be homogeneous of degree $D$, with coefficients chosen from $N$ before sampling. Consider $\gcd(N,F(\alpha))$. First divide $F$ by $Q=x_0^2+x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2$ as a monic polynomial in $x_0$. Take the gcd $g$ of $N$ and every remainder coefficient, using $g=N$ for zero remainder. Reduction modulo each $r\mid N$ commutes with this division. A proper $g$ already separates the primes. If $g=N$, then every sample returns $N$. If $g=1$, neither local polynomial is a multiple of $Q$. Dense coefficient arithmetic for this test takes polynomial time in $D$ and $\log N$ in four variables.

At either prime $r$ in the last case, the split matrix model identifies the projective quadric $Q=0$ with the rank-one matrices up to scale, parametrized by $A=uv^T$ on $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$. Substituting this parametrization into $F$ gives a nonzero biform of bidegree $(D,D)$ since the defining ideal of the Segre quadric is generated by the determinant. Fix $u$. At most $D$ exceptional $u$ make every coefficient in $v$ vanish, since at least one coefficient is a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree $D$ in $u$. Each exceptional fiber has $r+1$ cells, and each remaining fiber has at most $D$ roots in $v$. Thus at most $2D(r+1)$ projective cells are hit. Homogeneity makes vanishing independent of the scalar representing the cell. Each cell is a one-dimensional subspace with the origin removed, so the $c=3$ bound gives probability at most $O_\epsilon(D r^{-1/2+\epsilon})$. Summing over the two primes bounds the probability of a proper factor.

This applies directly to every point-dependent twist with homogeneous coordinates of common degree $k$ via $F(x)=\sum_jx_jV_j(x)$ and $D=k+1$, including singular twists. In particular, polylogarithmic degree and a polylogarithmic menu of such formulas still give vanishing success probability after the coefficient checks. The proof uses homogeneity to control a cell as a whole.

**The level-11 norm.** The same homogeneous-gcd conclusion holds for the uniform integer points of $Q_{11}(x,y,z,w)=x^2+xy+3y^2+z^2+zw+3w^2=pq$ when $pq$ is coprime to $22$. Squarefreeness makes each point primitive. The norm splits locally as the matrix determinant at both primes, so its projective quadric has exactly the same Segre parametrization. The public remainder test uses $Q_{11}$, monic in $x$. For the counting bound, positive definiteness gives $\|\alpha\|\ll\sqrt N$, so the same lattice back-substitution applies. A plane section of $Q_{11}=N$ is again empty, a point, or an ellipse with positive-definite binary restriction. The number of norm-$N$ points is at least $4(p+1)(q+1)/3$ by the level-11 theta identities. These facts give the same $O_\epsilon(D R^{-1/2+\epsilon})$ bound for $\gcd(N,F(\alpha))$. ## Several independent norm representations

Consider a polynomial circuit $F$ evaluated on $k$ independent uniform norm-$N$ points. Allow either of the two forms above in each block. Require $F$ to be homogeneous separately in each block, of degrees $d_i$, and put $D=\sum_i d_i$. Its coefficients can depend on $N$ and are fixed before the draws. The proper-gcd probability is $O_\epsilon(D R^{-1/2+\epsilon})$ whenever its restriction to the product of the projective quadrics is a nonzero polynomial at both primes. The constant is independent of $k$.

To prove this, work at one prime and substitute $u_i v_i^T$ for each projective matrix. Select one nonzero coefficient when the resulting polynomial is expanded in the last pair of line variables. By induction on $k$, this coefficient vanishes on the previous draws with probability at most $C_\epsilon(\sum_{i<k} d_i)r^{-1/2+\epsilon}$. Otherwise the last polynomial is nonzero, and independence gives the one-block bound $C_\epsilon d_k r^{-1/2+\epsilon}$. A coefficient remains homogeneous in each earlier pair of line variables, so the induction applies down to the constant case. Sum the bounds at the two primes.

**Testing the exception from $N$ alone.** A polynomial that vanishes identically at just one prime can already be used directly on modular matrices. In each block draw two independent primitive residue pairs modulo $N$ and form their outer-product matrix. Convert its entries to the norm coordinates using the public splitting and evaluate the circuit. At each prime this generates independent uniform projective cells. For a nonzero restriction the single-block zero probability is at most $2d_i/(r+1)$ by the same fiber count, and the preceding induction gives $2D/(r+1)$ for all blocks. Thus if $F$ is identically zero at one prime only, a single modular evaluation returns a proper gcd with probability at least $1-2D/(s+1)$, with $s$ the other prime. If the restriction vanishes at both primes, every integral or modular sample returns $N$. The modular test handles arithmetic circuits directly, even when expanding their coefficients would be expensive.

Consequently on balanced semiprimes, any such polynomial-degree circuit with an inverse-polynomial success probability on independent integral samples must, for sufficiently large inputs with that guarantee, have a one-sided identity. The modular test exploits it without drawing an integer representation. The hypotheses here are separate homogeneity, independence of the samples and a bound on total degree. The test script checks a planted two-block identity and enumerates a bilinear determinant over all pairs of projective cells at three primes. [Circuit probe and checks](check_quaternion_duality.py)

## How often a dense twist improves the odds

Let $V$ be uniform among residues with unit norm modulo $N$, independent of $\alpha$. For each fixed $\alpha$, its local action is a uniform element of $\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb F_r)$. It takes $\ker A$ to $\operatorname{im}A$ with probability $1/(r+1)$. The two local actions are independent by CRT, giving the exact mean proper-divisor probability above.

There is concentration even when a random twist is fixed once and reused. Let $t=r+1$, let $\mu_{ij}$ be the probability of kernel line $i$ and image line $j$ for $\alpha$, and put $X(G)=\sum_i\mu_{i,Gi}$. Each marginal is uniform: the point sampler chooses the image line uniformly, and conjugation exchanges kernel and image. Specifying both lines determines the nonzero matrix $A$ up to scale. Its possible residues lie in a subspace of codimension three, so $\max\mu_{ij}\le r^{-3/2+o(1)}$. The projective action is doubly transitive. In $X^2$ an individual cell is selected with probability $1/t$, and two cells in distinct rows and columns are both selected with probability $1/(t(t-1))$. The sum of their ordered cross-products is $1-2/t+\sum\mu_{ij}^2$. It follows that

$$\mathbb E X=1/t,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}X=\frac{\sum_{i,j}\mu_{ij}^2-1/t^2}{t-1}
\le r^{-5/2+o(1)}.$$

By Chebyshev, outside a fraction $r^{-1/4+o(1)}$ of twists the single-prime hit probability differs from $1/(r+1)$ by at most $r^{-1-1/8}$. The simultaneous-hit probability, averaged over twists, is $1/((p+1)(q+1))$. By Markov it exceeds $R^{-5/4}$ on at most $O(R^{-3/4})$ of twists. Consequently a fraction $1-R^{-1/4+o(1)}$ of twists have proper-divisor probability $(1/(p+1)+1/(q+1))(1+O(R^{-1/8}))$.
