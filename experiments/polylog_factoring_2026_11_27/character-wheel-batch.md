# Searching two halves of a character wheel

On $N=762201824107$, 373 continued-fraction steps gave four independent constraints on the quadratic characters of its prime divisors at conductor $m=2042040$. They allow 23,040 unit residues. The splitter represented these with two lists of lengths 192 and 120. One degree-192 polynomial at 240 points produced the verified divisor 935489. It used only $N$ and the collected rows. On the same input the single-class Harvey–Hittmeir search using only oddness used 159 baby steps and 218 giants, without the relation-collection stage. ## Factoring across the CRT split

Write $m=m_1m_2$ with coprime halves. Each unit residue is $u+v\bmod m$ for canonical contributions $0\le u,v<m$ supported on opposite halves. Restrict the binary row equations to each half by eliminating the other coordinates. Enumerate just the half-classes satisfying the projected equations and group them by their **partial parity** on each original row. A pair satisfies all the equations exactly when the two vectors of partial parities match. In particular this works when an equation involves primes on both halves.

For matching groups $A,B$, all possible smaller balanced primes occur among $u+v+tm$, with $u\in A$, $v\in B$. The publicly bounded interval for $t$ has length $K=O(1+\sqrt N/m)$, including the carry from adding two CRT contributions. If its first element is $t_0$, write $t=t_0+i+dj$ with $0\le i<d$. Use a monic product polynomial with roots $u+im$ modulo $N$ and evaluate at $-v-(t_0+dj)m$. A qualifying pair produces a factor. Padded final blocks and sums outside the balanced interval can produce incidental proper factors, which are also valid.

Remove duplicate roots modulo $N$. At a point equal to one of the remaining roots modulo the whole input, use the formal derivative, whose value is exactly the product of differences from all the *other* roots. This removes the unusable full-modulus equality even if many points need this treatment. A value with gcd $N$ can then be refined by one scan of that root list to get a proper gcd. The same proof covers degenerate input lists and repeated residues. [Earlier product-tree batching](submask-batch.md) · [Hittmeir, revealing subsets, Algorithm 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.16729)

Choose $d$ within a constant factor of $\sqrt{K|B|/|A|}$ clipped to $[1,K]$. The bit cost summed over matching groups is
$$
\operatorname{poly}(\log N,\log m)\sum_{A,B}\left(|A|+|B|+\sqrt{K|A||B|}\right),
$$
plus local table construction, polynomial in the sizes of the individual auxiliary primes. This charges creation of both half lists. If the row space has dimension $h$ and the subspaces supported wholly on either half have dimensions $h_1,h_2$, there are exactly $2^c$ matched parity vectors for $c=h-h_1-h_2$. Indeed these are the image of either projected sign space under the remaining $c$ independent linear forms. Surjectivity of the local character maps makes all fibers equal on either half. Writing $S$ for the full class count, the square-root terms sum to $2^{c/2}\sqrt{KS}$, quantifying the cost of equations crossing the chosen split. At the earlier 64-bit example with only 48 complete classes, the smaller conductor leaves a long lift interval. The first matching group needed degree 5,500 and 5,502 evaluations. The earlier Harvey–Hittmeir completion used 1,189 baby steps and 34 giants. [Comparison](cfrac-character-rows.md)

This CRT split and the product computations use classical techniques. Bernstein uses focused CRT enumeration to search local-square patterns in intervals. Legendre's factoring method already excludes candidate prime divisors by quadratic characters. Here the output is a factor from a collision between the two halves, and projections preserve arbitrary homogeneous parity rows supplied by the native collector. ## Cost of covering all compatible candidates

With quadratic labels alone the complete class count is $S=m^{1-o(1)}$ as $m$ grows. For polynomial $m\le\sqrt N$, even the square-root term for scheduling a *complete* batch is at least $\sqrt{KS}=N^{1/4-o(1)}$ when the full balanced range is used. For $m>\sqrt N$, the product of the two projection sizes is at least $S$. Constructing the larger list then already costs $m^{1/2-o(1)}$. This covers the tradeoff for this two-list method. An individual input can split early.

One may try to cover many classes by a primitive linear target directly. Fix $\gcd(a,b)=1$, an odd squarefree lock modulus $D$ coprime to $N$, and a residue $c$. If $aN/x+bx\equiv c\pmod D$ at a unit $x$, then $bx^2-cx+aN$ vanishes modulo every prime dividing $D$. This nonzero polynomial has at most two roots at each prime. For $t$ independent local signs at primes $\ell_i\equiv1\pmod4$, there are $\prod(\ell_i-1)/2^t$ admissible classes. A single target covers at most $2^t$. To attain the count, partition each allowed local set into pairs $u,v$. For any choice of a pair at each prime take $b=1$, $c=u+v$ and $a=uv/N$ locally, and combine $a,c$ by CRT. The resulting targets partition the admissible classes into groups of exactly $2^t$. At $D=5\cdot13\cdot17$, twelve targets cover 96 classes, eight per target.

There is a version for the actual balanced interval even when targets may use different subsets of a much larger conductor. Suppose every auxiliary prime is at most a fixed power of $\log N$. The homogeneous certificate equations admit every integer square $x=t^2$ coprime to the conductor. The prime number theorem gives $N^{1/4-o(1)}$ such $x$ in $[\sqrt{N/2},\sqrt N]$ by taking prime $t$. If a primitive target has squarefree lock $D\ge N^\delta$ for fixed $\delta>0$, let $\gamma=\min(\delta,1/4)$. Take a subproduct $N^\gamma\le D'\le N^\gamma\operatorname{poly}(\log N)$. The quartic $bt^4-ct^2+aN$ has at most $4^{\omega(D')}=N^{o(1)}$ roots modulo $D'$. At most $N^{1/4-\gamma+o(1)}$ of the test squares can pass this target. Thus covering the entire compatible interval by targets of that strength costs at least $N^{\gamma-o(1)}$ targets, regardless of whether any full CRT table is built. The scope is coverage of all compatible integers by those congruences. The test squares are easy to recognize. A method that uses primality, exact divisibility or a different target family can bypass this particular obstruction.
