# A Coppersmith window that separates two hidden ranks

On a balanced squarefree semiprime $N=pq$, $p<q\le2p$, one random center and one small-root calculation produce **independent** local bits with hit probabilities $W/p$ and $W/q$, where $W=2\lfloor N^{1/4}/8\rfloor+1$. We use them for two purposes. One sampler has exactly uniform local marginals and a known dependence between them. The other has genuinely independent components and samples subgroups with probability proportional to the square root of their size. Both use only $N$ and the requested dimension. ## Computing the two indicators

Set $X=(W-1)/2$. Given $x\bmod N$, return the product of the prime divisors $r$ for which $x+y\equiv0\pmod r$ for some $|y|\le X$. Either prime is at least $L=\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil$. Apply univariate small-root recovery to $f(Y)=x+Y$ with unknown divisor at least $L$. Every qualifying offset is a root of a single certified integer polynomial. Take the lcm of the verified gcds. If the window contains a multiple of $N$, both primes qualify and the answer is $N$.

Recovery of approximate multiples and the quarter-root radius are established results. Earlier work in the repository used this basis to decode a hint about one factor. Here we collect all qualifying components for any center and determine their joint law for random centers. Running the reader at several divisor sizes gives a splitting trial on every composite, including prime powers. [Multiscale proof](affine-divisor-scales.md) · [Howgrave-Graham on approximate gcds](https://cr.yp.to/bib/2001/howgrave-graham.pdf) · [Coppersmith on factoring with high bits known](https://cr.yp.to/bib/2001/coppersmith.pdf) · [May, Theorem 1.5](https://www.cits.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/imperia/md/content/may/paper/intro_to_coppersmiths_method.pdf) · [Earlier hint decoder](../../src/trackE/coppersmith.py) · [Prior repository analysis](../../notes/leakmap_results.md)

Use $D=2m$ coefficient columns with basis polynomials $N^{m-i}f^i$ for $0\le i<m$ and $Y^j f^m$ for $0\le j<m$. Scale column $j$ by $X^j$. The determinant is $N^{m(m+1)/2}X^{D(D-1)/2}$. Every row vanishes modulo $r^m$ at every qualifying offset. LLL and Cauchy–Schwarz certify all offsets whenever
$$\sqrt D\,2^{(D-1)/4}\big(N^{m(m+1)/2}X^{D(D-1)/2}\big)^{1/D}<L^m.$$
Put $B=\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ and $m=\max(4,\lceil B/8\rceil+2)$. Substituting $X\le N^{1/4}/8$ and dividing the left side by $(N/2)^{m/2}$ gives at most $\sqrt{2m}\,2^{5/4-2m}N^{1/8}<1$. The implementation searches smaller certified dimensions first, checks the actual row norm with integer arithmetic, and uses a primitive Sturm sequence and bisection to isolate integer roots. Radii up to two use direct gcds. Each center takes polynomial bit time.

The two residues of a uniform $x$ are independent and $W\le p$. Thus the returned divisor is proper with probability
$$\theta=\frac{W}{p}+\frac{W}{q}-\frac{2W^2}{N}=\Theta(N^{-1/4}).$$
An ordinary factor search can also keep the individual gcds when both primes qualify at different offsets. There are exactly $W(W-1)$ such centers by CRT, one for every ordered pair of distinct offsets. This improves the per-center probability by $W(W-1)/N$, of smaller order than $\theta$. The rank constructions use the product because it specifies exactly the marked prime components.

At $N=335544310331639$ a window has 1,069 positions. At three recorded centers a dimension-nine lattice calculation tested one candidate offset to find $16777213$, zero for an empty window, and two for a window meeting both primes at distinct offsets. These are planted controls. The stated probability follows from CRT. [Exact checks](check_affine_prime_window.py)

### What a better choice of centers has to accomplish

For a fixed $N$, let $A_N$ be the centers at which the reader returns a proper divisor. Its exact size is $N\theta$. Given any pool of $t$ centers with uniform marginals, the chance that even one center works is at most $t\theta$, regardless of dependence or how a selector ranks the pool. More generally, if every center assigns at most $K/N$ probability to each residue, the bound becomes $tK\theta$ by summing over $A_N$. Achieving inverse-polynomial success with polynomially many centers on a $b$-bit balanced input therefore requires $K\ge 2^{b/4}/\mathrm{poly}(b)$. Concentrating the sampler is only a necessary condition. It has to concentrate on intervals meeting a multiple of exactly one hidden prime. This gives a stopping rule for variants that merely reorder uniform proposals.

A concentrated center can still miss systematically. For any particular $N=pq$, use $x=a/b\bmod N$ with $b$ invertible. If $|a|+|b|X<p$, then $r\mid a+by$ for $|y|\le X$ and $r\in\{p,q\}$ requires $a+by=0$ as an integer. Both primes then qualify together. This rules out every such center even when the rule for choosing $a,b$ depends on the whole input.

There is also an average bound for much larger numerators and denominators. Take distinct primes uniformly from $[R,2R]$ and fix a menu of $M$ fractions before drawing them. Put $U=\lfloor\sqrt{2R}/8\rfloor$, so $X(pq)\le U$, and let $D$ bound the bit lengths of all denominators and nonzero values $a+by$ for $|y|\le U$. Every proper denominator gcd or split from a window requires a prime from the pair to divide at least one of these nonzero integers. A zero term in the actual window makes both primes qualify. Each nonzero integer has at most $D/\log_2 R$ distinct prime divisors in the band. Since there are $\Theta(R/\log R)$ primes to choose from, the probability that *any* menu entry succeeds is at most $O(M(2U+2)D/R)$. Thus even adaptively choosing among polynomially many fixed fractions of polynomial bit length gives mean success at most $R^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{poly}(\log R)$, the same exponential scale as random centers. Evaluating a fixed menu of integer polynomial fractions $A(N)/B(N)$ reduces to their constant terms modulo $N$ whenever division is defined. A fresh center selected using the digits of $N$ can fall outside this bound. The repository used the same large-prime-divisor counting principle for an earlier powering test with a different set of witness integers. ## Uniform at each prime in odd dimension

Let $d=2k+1$. A uniform local subspace has rank weights given by the Gaussian binomials. A uniform local projector has weights $|\mathrm{GL}_d|/(|\mathrm{GL}_j||\mathrm{GL}_{d-j}|)$. Both laws are symmetric in $j,d-j$. Rank parity is a fair bit independent of the distance $T=k-\min(j,d-j)$ from the two middle ranks. Parity and distance together specify the rank. [Local counts](projector-sampling.md)

Flip one fair global bit by each of the two window indicators. The local parities are individually fair and disagree with probability $\theta$. Draw the two distances independently of one another and of the parities. For subspaces the ratio of weights at ranks $j-1,j$ below the middle is $(r^j-1)/(r^{d-j+1}-1)\le r^{-2}$ for $j\le k$. For projectors the ratio has a further factor $r^{-(d-2j+1)}\le r^{-2}$. Summing the geometric bounds and normalizing shows that the probability of nonzero distance is at most $r^{-2}$ for subspaces and $r^{-4}$ for projectors.

The distance correction uses one independent uniform integer guess in $I=[\lceil\sqrt{N/2}\rceil,\lfloor\sqrt{2N}\rfloor]$. It equals one of the two factors with probability $h=2/|I|\ge 1/p\ge2/p^2$. This covers the total probability that either distance is nonzero. On a miss set both to zero. On a hit the complete local tables are known. Assign each noncentral pair its intended mass divided by $h$ and assign the remaining probability to $(0,0)$. Averaging over the guess gives the exact product law. A uniform change of basis then gives independent uniform orientations within the chosen ranks.

Only the parity pair differs from the unrestricted uniform law. The exact total variation distance is $|1/2-\theta|$. On odd inputs the determinant of $I-2E$ exposes a divisor on parity disagreement. The index or rank decoder also splits on equal parities and unequal distances. Its success probability is $1-(1-\theta)\sum_t\Pr(T_p=t)\Pr(T_q=t)$. At $N=67\cdot71$ the width is 3, $\theta=396/4757$, and the distance is $3965/9514$. [Matrix and lattice checks](check_coupled_rank_sampler.py)

## Independent components under square-root size weighting

Give each subgroup $H\subseteq(\mathbb Z/N)^d$ weight $\sqrt{|H|}$. The target is a product distribution across the two primes. Its local rank weights are
$$v_r(j)=\binom{d}{j}_{\!r}\,r^{j/2}.$$
The most likely rank is $k+1$. Relative to that rank, the weight of $k$ is $r^{-1/2}$ and the weight of $k+2$, when present, is at most $r^{-3/2}$. Farther down, successive ratios are at most $r^{-5/2}$, and farther up they are at most $r^{-3/2}$. Thus the probability $\delta_r$ of any other rank is at most $2/\sqrt r$ for $r\ge257$, uniformly in $d$.

Handle prime divisors at most 256 by constant-bounded trial division. Otherwise try at most 96 independent public windows, stopping at the first proper divisor. The probability of obtaining the factors is $h=1-(1-\theta)^{96}$, which can be calculated exactly once a window succeeds. Put $S=1/\sqrt p+1/\sqrt q$. The width gives $\theta\ge S/16$. Indeed $W\ge N^{1/4}/8$, $N^{1/4}/r\ge 2^{-1/4}/\sqrt r$ for either prime, and at $p\ge257$ the overlap removes less than one quarter of the sum of the two hit probabilities. Since $S\le1/8$, we obtain $h\ge96\theta/(1+96\theta)\ge2S\ge\delta_p+\delta_q$.

On a miss use rank $k+1$ at both primes. On a hit assign every pair outside this common center its true mass divided by $h$ and give the remainder to the center. The rank probabilities can involve both square roots. The implementation compares cumulative weights exactly in the basis $1,\sqrt p,\sqrt q,\sqrt{pq}$, then draws uniformly in the two resulting orbits. This gives the exact square-root-weighted law with a fixed cap on window calls. A second construction extends the law to all moduli and dimensions with a polynomial expected cost. [All-modulus extension](weighted-sublattices-general.md) · [Sampler](sqrt_weighted_sublattice.py) · [Checks](check_sqrt_weighted_sublattice.py)

For the related weights $|H|^\gamma$, $0\le\gamma\le1/2$, the ratio of the two middle weights is $r^\gamma$. All other ranks total $O(r^{-3/2})$ times the combined weight of those two, uniformly in $d$. For balanced primes the probability of different middle ranks is of order $p^{-\gamma}$. Thus a sampler at $\gamma=O(\log\log N/\log N)$ would yield a factor in polynomially many trials. The implemented value $\gamma=1/2$ requires order $N^{1/4}$ trials.

## What already follows from a general factoring algorithm

On balanced semiprimes with odd $d$ and fixed rational $0<\gamma\le1/2$, let $\delta$ be the probability that at least one prime has rank different from $k+1$. The rank ratios above give $\delta=O(N^{-\gamma/2})$, uniformly in $d$. Conversely, the probability that the two ranks differ is $\Theta(N^{-\gamma/2})$. Dimension one already shows both scales. The four possible subgroups have respective weights $1,p^\gamma,q^\gamma,(pq)^\gamma$. Exactly the two middle ones have an index that splits $N$.

Take any factorer with expected cost $T(N)$ and open a public random branch with dyadic probability $h=\Theta(N^{-\gamma/2})$ large enough to dominate $\delta$, capped at one. One can compute such a probability from the bit length and $\gamma$ alone. When the branch opens, factor $N$ and give each nondefault pair its true probability divided by $h$. Otherwise return the default ranks. Exact cumulative comparisons with known primes use real algebraic numbers of bounded degree for fixed rational $\gamma$. The resulting exact sampler costs $\mathrm{poly}(d,\log N)+O(N^{-\gamma/2}T(N))$ in expectation. In the other direction, independent draws of expected cost $S$ recover a divisor at expected cost $O((S+\mathrm{poly}(d,\log N))N^{\gamma/2})$. These statements charge the factor searches even when they run inside a sampler.

In particular, Harvey and Hittmeir's deterministic $N^{1/5}\,\mathrm{poly}(\log N)$ bound already gives an expected-polynomial sampler for $2/5\le\gamma\le1/2$. Lenstra and Pomerance's unconditional expected $N^{o(1)}$ factoring bound does the same for every fixed rational $0<\gamma\le1/2$. These are deductions from the published factoring bounds. The Coppersmith construction at $\gamma=1/2$ caps the number of window searches at 96 on every draw, each with polynomial bit cost. Ordinary exact random sampling still has variable runtime. Lowering the weight by occasionally invoking an existing factorer reproduces its factoring cost when the sampler is used repeatedly.
